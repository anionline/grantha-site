If you grew up on Hergé’s Tintin, you know the two best books in the series. Destination Moon and Explorers on the Moon, 1953 and 1954. A red-and-white checked rocket stands on its tail on the Moon, and then it brings everyone home.

More than sixty years later, Elon Musk was asked why his new spaceship looked so much like it. His answer: “If in doubt, go with Tintin.”

Replay is the point.

SpaceX did not build the first reusable rocket. The Space Shuttle flew again and again for thirty years. It still cost about $28,000 to put a pound in orbit.

What SpaceX built was a rocket you can replay. The booster lands, gets checked, refuels and flies again. One Falcon 9 booster has now flown 37 times. On Falcon 9, a pound in orbit costs about $1,200. On Falcon Heavy, about $640.

That changed the structure of the whole industry. In 2025 SpaceX flew 165 Falcon 9 missions, one every two or three days. It put more than 3,000 Starlink satellites in orbit, about eight a day.

Starlink is just the first product we can see. Ideas that were too expensive to try are now on real drawing boards: data centers in orbit, mining in space, factories that need no gravity.

When something becomes replayable, the ecosystem around it changes shape.

AI today is pre-SpaceX rocketry

What does that have to do with AI?

Here is what happens when you ask an AI assistant a question. It searches what it can reach. It pulls back what looks relevant and reads it into its working memory, page by page. It works out what it says, decides which parts answer you, and writes a reply.

Every page read and every step of thought costs tokens.

Now ask the same question tomorrow. Or have a colleague ask it. The whole flight runs again: search, read, reason, write. What the AI worked out the first time is not there for the next flight. It is a single-use rocket.

Single-use

Every unnecessary part on a rocket is weight it has to lift. Every unnecessary step in AI is tokens someone has to pay for. Today’s AI reads whole documents to find one paragraph. It reads the same documents again for the next question, and again for the one after that.

Ask Copilot, ChatGPT and Claude the same question and you may get three different answers. Each one searches differently, finds different documents, and weighs them differently. Ask the same AI twice and the answer can change, because each answer is written fresh.

The answer is delivered and the work behind it is thrown away. The company paid for all that reading and keeps none of it. Tomorrow it pays again.

The question nobody asked

Rocketry didn’t change because someone built a better throwaway rocket. It changed because someone asked why we throw rockets away.

AI is at that moment now. Every answer is a rocket built, flown once and dropped in the sea.

We’re asking that question from first principles. The work an AI does once should land where every AI can find it, and fly again.

SpaceX had its moment the day a booster came home, and then flew again and again and again. AI hasn’t had that moment yet. SeaSand exists to bring it.
