Name it after the closest thing people already know, and they will hear the old thing. Give it a word nobody has heard before, and they won’t hear anything at all. I’ve built companies in documents for twenty years, and I’m in the middle of this problem again right now. So this post is about the dilemma itself, and how other people got out of it.

The closest old name is a trap

The first cars were called horseless carriages. The name told you exactly what the thing was, and exactly how to judge it: as a carriage, minus the horse. People asked whether it was as reliable as a horse, and missed that it would rebuild cities.

Radio started life as the wireless: a telegraph without the wire. The name made it sound like a cheaper telegram, and hid that it was something new, one voice reaching millions at once.

Old names are comfortable because they come with a mental model. That’s also the problem. The reader takes the old model, ticks the boxes it already knows, and files your idea next to the thing it replaces.

It happens in our own field too. The computer “file” was named after the paper folder, and the file system after the filing cabinet. That was a gift in its day: everyone understood it at once. Fifty years later, the metaphor still decides how we store knowledge, even now that machines do more and more of the reading.

A brand-new word is a trap too

So you go the other way. You invent a word, clean and unclaimed, with nothing attached to it.

Now you have the opposite problem. A new word is an empty box. Nobody knows what goes in it, and every reader has to be taught, one at a time. Big companies can afford years of that teaching. A startup gets one sentence, maybe two, before the listener decides whether to keep listening.

So the founder is stuck between a name that is understood and wrong, and a name that is right and not understood.

The way through: borrow a reference, not a category

The names that worked did something subtle. They borrowed an old picture without borrowing the old category.

The electronic spreadsheet took its name from the accountant’s paper sheet, ruled into rows and columns. Everyone could picture it. But nobody mistook it for paper, because the picture came with something paper could never do: change one number and watch every other one move.

The desktop, the window and the folder on your screen worked the same way. Each one borrowed an object from the office so you’d know where to start, then quietly did things no office object could.

So the trick isn’t old name or new name. It’s a reference that teaches. Pick an image people already hold, and choose one that carries the new idea inside it, so the more they think about the picture, the closer they get to what you’re building.

Our turn: sand, oysters and pearls

We’re in this exact spot at SeaSand. The closest old name for what we’re building is one everyone knows, and it arrives with fifty years of assumptions. A brand-new word would be all ours, and would mean nothing to anyone.

So we went to the sea.

Every company’s knowledge starts as sea sand: countless grains, scattered, mostly overlooked. A grain of sand on its own isn’t worth much. But put one inside an oyster and give it time, and layer by layer it becomes a pearl.

That’s the picture we chose. We call the way we build the Oyster architecture, and what we hand to AI, Pearls. Nobody will mistake an oyster for a filing cabinet. And the longer you sit with the picture (a raw grain, patient layering, something of lasting value at the end), the closer you get to what we’re doing.

We’ll admit to the smile, too. It’s hard to be called SeaSand and not end up with pearls.

We haven’t solved this, and we’d love your help

This is the messaging problem we’re living with every day. We know the rule we want to follow: resist the closest old name, because it will be judged against the thing it replaces. Resist the empty new word too, because we don’t have years to teach it. Look for a reference people already carry, one that points forward. Knowing the rule is the easy part. Living it, in one sentence, to a busy reader, is the hard part.

Oysters and Pearls are our first answer. We’re sure there are better ones out there, and better ways to tell the story.
